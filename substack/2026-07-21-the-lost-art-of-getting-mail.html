Ask any kid what's more exciting: a notification on a screen, or an envelope with their own name on it, found in an actual mailbox. Almost every child picks the envelope, and almost every adult watching is a little surprised by how much they still mean it.

Why physical mail still lands differently

A notification is abundant and disposable. A piece of mail addressed specifically to a child is rare and tangible: it took someone's time, a stamp, an actual walk to a postbox. A child can hold it, keep it, put it somewhere, come back and reread it days later. It doesn't disappear into a feed. And because physical mail takes days to arrive, it comes with something most digital messages skip entirely: real anticipation.

What it's quietly teaching, without trying to

More than it looks like

  • Delayed gratification: waiting days for something to arrive is a genuinely rare experience for a lot of kids now.
  • The idea that someone thought of them specifically, in advance, on purpose. Not a mass notification, a decision.
  • An early, low-pressure form of writing: replying to a postcard is often a child's first unprompted writing project, because it doesn't feel like an assignment.

Easy ways to bring it back

Send postcards on ordinary days, not just from holidays. The unexpected version tends to land better than the expected one. Let a young child dictate a reply or just scribble one; there's no "good enough" handwriting requirement here. And keep a small box for the ones that arrive. Kids will reread an old postcard as happily as a new one, which is more than can be said for most notifications.

A postcard doesn't need to compete with anything. It just needs a mailbox and a bit of patience.

Frequently asked questions

Why do kids love getting mail?

A letter or postcard with their name on it took someone's time and effort, can be held and kept, and arrives after days of anticipation, unlike a disposable notification.

What does getting mail teach children?

Delayed gratification, the feeling that someone thought of them on purpose, and an early, low-pressure form of writing through replying.

How can I bring postcards back into my child's life?

Send them on ordinary days, let a young child dictate or scribble a reply, and keep a small box for the ones that arrive.

Does my child need neat handwriting to reply?

No. There is no good-enough standard for a postcard reply. A drawing or a scribble is a perfectly good answer.
