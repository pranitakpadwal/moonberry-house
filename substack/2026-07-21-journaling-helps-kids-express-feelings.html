How Journaling Helps Kids Who Can't Say What They're Feeling Yet

Young children often feel something clearly and have no idea how to say it out loud. Here's why a page can reach a feeling that a direct question can't, and how to start.

Quick answer: Journaling helps young children because a page removes the audience. Drawing or writing in private lowers the pressure to find the right words for a feeling. Start with a specific prompt like drawing the best five minutes of the day, let drawing count as journaling, and treat the page as theirs.

Ask a five-year-old why they're upset and you'll often get a shrug, or an answer that's clearly not the real one. It's not evasion. Naming a feeling is a genuinely late-developing skill: a child can feel something with total clarity years before they have the words to hand it to someone else.

Why "how do you feel?" so often doesn't work

A direct question about feelings asks a young child to do several hard things at once: notice the feeling, label it correctly, and then perform that label out loud to an adult who's watching and waiting. That's a lot of steps for a nervous system that's still building the vocabulary for any of it. A page doesn't watch and wait. It just sits there, which turns out to matter more than it sounds like it should.

What a page can reach that a conversation sometimes can't

Drawing or writing in a journal removes the audience from the equation, and removing the audience often removes the performance pressure along with it. A child who freezes when asked "what's wrong?" will frequently draw or scribble something that gets at the actual thing, simply because nobody's face is waiting for the right answer. It's not that the journal is magic. It's that it's private, unhurried, and entirely theirs.

How to actually start, without making it a chore

  • Skip "how was your day." A specific prompt, like "draw the best five minutes of today", gets further than an open-ended question most children don't know how to answer.
  • Let drawing count as journaling. The words can come later, or never. The page doing its job doesn't require handwriting.
  • Don't read it as a report. Treat it as theirs, not as a status update you're entitled to review line by line.
The point was never to get the feeling onto the page correctly. It's to give it somewhere to go at all.

Frequently asked questions

Why won't my child tell me how they feel?

Naming a feeling is a late-developing skill. Being asked to notice, label and say a feeling out loud to a waiting adult is a lot for a young child, so a shrug is common and not evasion.

How does journaling help children express feelings?

A page does not watch or wait. Drawing or writing in private takes away the performance pressure, so children often get closer to the real feeling than they can in conversation.

What can my child write in a journal if they can't write yet?

Drawing counts. Words can come later or never. A specific prompt such as drawing the best five minutes of today works better than asking how the day was.

Should I read my child's journal?

It works best when it feels like theirs. Treat it as a private space rather than a status report, and let them choose what to share.
