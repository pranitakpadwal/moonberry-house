What Your Kid's Drawings Are Actually Telling You

A scribble isn't nothing, and it isn't a hidden message either. Here's a grounded look at what children's drawings really reveal at different ages, and what they usually don't.

Quick answer: Most of what shows up in a child's drawing reflects their age and motor development, not their mood. Size, placement and repetition across many drawings say more than colour choice or one odd detail. The best move is to ask your child to tell you about the picture before interpreting it.

A fridge fills up with them faster than you can rotate them out: scribbles, suns with too many rays, a family where someone's arms are longer than their whole body. It's tempting to read them like tea leaves. The honest answer is a little less dramatic than that, and considerably more useful.

What drawing stages usually tell you (it's mostly about age, not mood)

Most of what shows up on the page in the early years tracks motor and cognitive development far more than emotional state. Scribbling gives way to basic shapes, shapes combine into a recognisable figure (usually a head with legs coming straight off it before a torso shows up at all), and detail accumulates gradually after that. A drawing that looks "behind" a peer's is almost always just a different pace of the same sequence, not a red flag.

Where a drawing does say something real

Size, placement, and repetition are usually more informative than colour choice or a single odd detail. A figure drawn much larger than everyone else in the picture, or consistently left out of family scenes, is worth a gentle, curious question, not a diagnosis. The size of the person, the space they take up on the page, who's close to whom: these tend to reflect how a child is currently experiencing their world far more reliably than which crayon they reached for.

What usually isn't worth reading into

  • Colour choice on its own. A black sun is very often just the crayon within reach, not a mood.
  • One unusual drawing. Patterns across many drawings mean far more than a single picture.
  • "Wrong" proportions. Almost always developmental, not emotional.
The most useful question isn't "what does this mean?" It's "tell me about this one", and then actually listening to the answer.

The one thing worth doing every time

Ask about the drawing before offering an interpretation of it. Children are often perfectly willing to explain exactly what's happening in a picture, in detail, if you ask, and their explanation is a far better source than any guess about symbolism. The drawing is rarely the message. The conversation about it usually is.

Frequently asked questions

Do children's drawings reveal their emotions?

Sometimes, but less than people think. In the early years, drawings mostly reflect motor and thinking development. Patterns across many drawings, such as size, placement and who is included, are more informative than a single picture or colour choice.

Does a child's colour choice mean something?

Usually not on its own. A black sun is very often just the crayon within reach. Colour choice is among the least reliable things to read into.

What should I say when my child shows me a drawing?

Ask them to tell you about it, then listen. Children can often explain exactly what is happening in a picture, and their explanation is a far better guide than any guess about symbolism.

When is a drawing worth a closer look?

If a figure is consistently much larger than everyone else, or a person is repeatedly left out of family scenes, a gentle curious question is worthwhile. It is a prompt for conversation, not a diagnosis. If you are worried, speak to your paediatrician or a school counsellor.
