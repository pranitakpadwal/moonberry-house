Sensory-Friendly Colouring: Choosing Crayons, Paper and Spaces

Published 22 November 20265 min readBy The Moonberry Desk

Home › Journal › Every Kind of Mind
Quick answer

To make colouring sensory-friendly, let the child choose their tool, use soft cream or pastel paper taped down, set up a quiet, softly lit space, keep the routine predictable, and drop any pressure to finish. Every child is different, so offer options and follow their lead.

Colouring looks like the simplest activity in the world: paper, crayon, go. But for a child who's sensitive to texture, sound, smell or light, it can be surprisingly hard. The waxy feel of a crayon, the squeak of a marker, the glare of white paper under a bright lamp: small things for some children are genuinely uncomfortable for others.

The good news is that a few thoughtful choices can turn colouring from a struggle into one of the calmest parts of the day. Every child is different, so treat everything below as a menu, not a rule.

Let the child choose the tool

Offer two or three options instead of a whole box, and let your child pick. Some children love the smooth glide of a gel crayon. Others can't bear the feel of wax on their fingers and prefer pencils or markers. Being in charge of the tool is already half the comfort.

  • Chunky crayons are easier to hold and need less pressure.
  • Colour pencils are quiet, with no smell and no mess.
  • Washable markers give strong colour with almost no effort, but some children dislike the sound or smell, so test with one before buying a full set.
  • Paint dabbers or stamps can be a gentle option for children who avoid gripping.

Think about the paper

Bright white paper can be glaring. Cream or very light pastel paper is softer on the eyes. Thicker paper feels sturdier and doesn't tear when a child presses hard. If your child dislikes the feel of loose sheets, tape the page to the table or use a clipboard so it doesn't slide around.

Shape the space

  • Light: soft, steady light is easier than harsh overhead light or flickering bulbs.
  • Sound: a quieter corner helps, and some children enjoy soft background music while others want silence. Ask them.
  • Seating: a comfortable, supported seat, or the floor, or a standing table. Let them move if they need to.
  • Mess: if mess is stressful, keep wipes and a damp cloth within reach, and use a tray or mat so the "mess zone" is clearly bounded.

Make it predictable

Many children relax when they know what to expect. Say how long colouring time is ("until the song ends", or "one page"), show them the page first, and keep the setup similar each time. A predictable routine takes away the worry of "what happens next", which leaves more room to enjoy the colours.

Remove the pressure to finish

There's no right way to colour a page. A page that's half done, scribbled over, or one single beautiful blue circle is a complete piece of work. Praise the effort and the colours they chose, not the neatness. If a child wants to stop, that's information worth respecting, not a failure.

A quick starter kit

  • 2–3 tool options (chunky crayon, pencil, washable marker)
  • Softly coloured or cream paper, taped down
  • A quiet, well-lit corner
  • Wipes within reach
  • A clear, gentle "finish line" (one page, or one song)

Frequently asked questions

What are the best crayons for a child with sensory sensitivities?

There is no single best choice. Chunky crayons, colour pencils and washable markers are all worth offering, because some children dislike the feel of wax while others dislike the sound or smell of markers. Let your child pick.

Why does white paper bother some children?

Bright white paper can look glaring under strong light. Cream or very light pastel paper is often softer on the eyes, and it costs about the same.

How can I make colouring less stressful for a child who hates mess?

Use a tray or mat to mark a clear mess zone, keep wipes within reach, and choose pencils or washable markers. Predictable setup and a clear finish line, such as one page or one song, also help.

Should I talk to a professional about my child's sensory needs?

If sensitivities are affecting daily life, an occupational therapist or your child's doctor can offer personalised guidance. These ideas are meant to sit alongside that support, not replace it.
