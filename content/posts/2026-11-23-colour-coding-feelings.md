A four-year-old who's furious and a four-year-old who's frightened can look exactly the same from the outside: red-faced, loud, in tears. Inside, they're very different, but they usually can't tell you which one it is. They don't have the words yet.

Colour can be a bridge. It's concrete, it's visual and it asks for no vocabulary. Pointing at a colour is much easier than finding the word "disappointed".

## Make a feelings chart together

Sit down with paper and crayons and ask your child: *"If happy were a colour, which colour would it be?"* Then try sad, angry, worried, excited, calm. Let them choose. There are no correct answers, and if their anger is blue, then in this house anger is blue.

Draw a small circle for each feeling, colour it in and write the word beside it. Stick the chart where your child can see it, at their eye level, not yours.

## Use it in the moment, lightly

When a big feeling arrives, try:

- "Which colour feels closest to how your body feels right now?"
- "Can you show me with the crayon?"
- "Is it a big splash of that colour or a little bit?"

Notice what you're *not* doing: you're not demanding an explanation or asking "why". You're making it possible for them to show you first. Words often follow once the feeling has a shape.

## Add size and mix

As children get comfortable, they can colour a body outline to show where each feeling lives: a red patch in the tummy, a blue one in the throat. They can also mix: "I'm a bit orange and a bit grey today." It teaches the lovely idea that feelings overlap and that we can feel more than one thing at once.

## Name it yourself, too

Children learn emotional language from hearing it used. Try saying, calmly and without drama, "I'm feeling a bit grey and tired today, so I'm going to sit quietly for a few minutes." You're showing that feelings are normal, nameable and manageable.

## A word of care

This is an everyday tool for helping children express themselves, not a way to diagnose anything. Don't interpret a child's colour choices as "meaning" something hidden. A child who picks black for happy isn't a worry; they might just love black. If a child seems persistently distressed, anxious or withdrawn, talk to your paediatrician or a school counsellor.

:::tips Try it this week
- Make the chart on a Sunday afternoon, together.
- Use it once a day at a calm moment, not only when things are hard.
- Let your child add new feelings and new colours whenever they like.
- Keep it playful. If they lose interest, put it away and try again another day.
:::

Naming a feeling doesn't make it vanish, but it makes it smaller and less scary, and it tells a child that whatever they feel, they're not alone with it.
